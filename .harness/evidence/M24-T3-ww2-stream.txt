data: {"text":"The official German narrative regarding World War II centered heavily on the Treaty of Versailles as the primary catalyst for conflict, specifically citing the requirement for Germany to accept 'sole responsibility' for the war as a central grievance that delegitimized the agreement and fueled national resentment [2]. German perspectives argued that the treaty's punitive measures created the instability necessary for the rise of extremist ideologies, contributing to the economic, political, and ideological environment that made World War II possible [2]. Consequently, German foreign policy framed the reversal of the treaty's territorial and military constraints as a necessary act of national restoration, aiming to undo the military restrictions and reverse the territorial losses imposed by the Treaty of Versailles [3]. Regarding territorial expansionism, the Nazi regime justified its actions by claiming the need to unify ethnic Germans living outside Germany's borders into a single state, seeking to 'incorporate territories with ethnic German populations into the Reich' [3]. The regime legitimized this expansion by distinguishing between historically German territories known as the 'Altreich' (Old Reich) and newly acquired lands, framing annexation as a restoration of historical boundaries [6]. Formal legal incorporation was used to present these actions as lawful integrations rather than conquest, with Austria, the Sudetenland, the Memel Territory, parts of Poland, and Eupen-Malmedy officially and permanently incorporated into the German Reich by law [6]. In territories under a 'Chief of Civil Administration', the regime justified de facto annexation as a necessary transitional phase toward full legal integration to achieve a gradual, seamless alignment with the Reich ahead of later official integration [6]. Expansion was also legitimized through a racial criterion where the local population was granted Reich citizenship provided they were classified as being of 'German descent' [6]. To coerce neighboring states into accepting these annexations, the Nazi regime utilized the explicit threat of military force as a justification and tool, noting that 'Germany then moved to acquire new territories using the threat of war' [3]. Ideologically, the expansion into Eastern Europe was justified by the core tenet of Nazism that Germany needed 'living space' (Lebensraum), creating a 'pull towards the East' (Drang nach Osten) deemed vital for the survival of the German nation where it could be found and colonized [6]. The leadership's perspective was further driven by geopolitical theories suggesting that controlling Eastern Europe was a prerequisite for global hegemony, summarized by the maxim: 'Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland. Who rules the Heartland commands the World-Island. Who rules the World-Island commands the World' [5]. Additionally, Nazi ideology viewed global economic organization as a mechanism for establishing Nordic racial dominance, asserting that 'the Nordic race undertakes its control' while other countries produce what they can best produce [5]. The leadership justified conquest through a belief that culturally superior but less ruthless races had to limit their increase against more brutal and natural peoples who would otherwise expand without limit [5]. Ultimately, the ideology held that national prosperity and survival were entirely dependent on the state's willingness to use aggressive military force as a tool of policy, stating that 'there can be no economic life unless behind this economic life there stands the determined political will of the nation absolutely ready to strike—and to strike hard' [5]."}
