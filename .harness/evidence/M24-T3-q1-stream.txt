Raft's architecture decomposes consensus into three independent sub-problems: leader election, log replication, and safety, which improves understandability compared to the more abstract nature of Paxos [1]. In contrast, traditional Paxos variants lack specific guidance on key operational components such as managing the log structure, handling leader failure, performing log compaction, and managing membership changes [2]. This underspecification in Paxos forces implementers to make many design choices independently, resulting in diverse implementations that are difficult to compare or reason about operationally [2]. While both algorithms achieve similar performance in steady state, Raft provides explicit algorithms for all aspects of consensus, whereas Multi-Paxos requires implementers to fill in substantial gaps [2]. Consequently, the real difference is whether a human can read the spec and build it without quietly introducing a bug that corrupts your data months later [4].\n\nRegarding implementation complexity, Raft was designed explicitly for understandability and practical implementation, lowering the barrier for correct implementation by breaking consensus into modular concerns [2]. Implementing correct Paxos requires solving complex edge cases around multiple proposers and failure handling that are not fully defined in the original protocol [3]. The reduced complexity of Raft has led to its adoption as the standard for new systems, including etcd, Consul, and TiKV [3]. Despite being the mathematical foundation of consensus, Paxos presents significant practical challenges that make it difficult to implement correctly without errors [3]. Raft is preferable when operational simplicity and clarity are prioritized over theoretical performance gains, as both algorithms offer identical safety and speed guarantees [4].\n\nIn terms of specific use cases, Raft is the preferred choice when operational constraints prioritize ease of implementation and understandability [5]. For standard use cases like leader election or distributed locks, neither algorithm should be implemented manually; instead, established libraries implementing these algorithms are the preferred solution, such as etcd, ZooKeeper, or Consul [4]. Within internal cluster management where nodes fail silently rather than act maliciously, Raft or Paxos (crash-fault tolerance) is the appropriate choice over Byzantine fault tolerance mechanisms [4]. However, Paxos is often used in systems where high reliability and fault tolerance are critical, making it preferable for specific use cases like distributed lock services requiring strong consistency guarantees [5]. Google Chubby, a distributed lock service ensuring coordination among distributed systems, is a notable example of Paxos usage [5]. Additionally, distributed storage systems requiring consistent write propagation utilize Paxos, as seen in Microsoft Azure Storage [5]. The original paper describes a 'single decree' Paxos which Multi-Paxos extends to a log of values, but the protocol remains underspecified regarding operational details [3]. Every production 'Paxos' system, such as Google's Chubby and ZooKeeper's Zab, is actually a custom variant because the core idea involves handling multiple proposers and failures in ways not fully defined by the original protocol [3]."}
