In October 2026, the estimated gross installation cost for an air-source heat pump in a typical UK home is between £8,000 and £14,000 [2], while standard gas boiler replacements range from £2,200 to £4,500 [2]. However, the Boiler Upgrade Scheme (BUS) provides a grant of £7,500 for properties in England and Wales, which is applied directly by accredited installers [1][2]. After applying this standard grant, the net installation cost for heat pumps typically falls between £2,500 and £6,500 [2], making it often comparable to the cost of a new boiler. For homes currently heated by oil, the grant increases to £9,000, resulting in a net heat pump cost of just £1,000 to £5,000 [2].

Regarding running costs, the unit price for electricity from October 3, 2026, is confirmed at 26.32p per kWh [4], whereas natural gas costs approximately 7.97p per kWh under the price cap for Direct Debit customers [3]. Gas remains roughly one-third of the price of electricity per unit [3]. Based on these rates, a gas boiler operating at full capacity (24 kW) costs approximately £1.91 to run per hour [3]. Because a heat pump typically requires less energy input to deliver the same amount of heat due to its efficiency, the higher electricity rate significantly impacts the comparison; specifically, the lower unit price of gas compared to electricity is a key factor in running cost differences [3].

In terms of performance during cold weather, modern air-source heat pumps maintain a Coefficient of Performance (COP) above 2.0 even at temperatures as low as -10°C [5]. At -5°C, the COP is specifically recorded at 2.2 [6], while at milder autumn temperatures of 7°C outside, a typical heat pump achieves a COP of 3.8 [6]. Heat pumps are equipped with built-in auxiliary heaters that engage only if the system drops 2°C below the setpoint, a condition that occurs for a few hours per year at most [5]. Additionally, while the defrost cycle required to clear ice from the outdoor unit reduces annual efficiency by approximately 3-5% [6], backup heating is rarely required as modern units can sustain output without significant reliance on auxiliary power [5]. Since the UK climate rarely sees prolonged sub-zero temperatures [6], the low-temperature reliability features of these units are well-matched to actual weather conditions.
